Manual S Equipment Selection After the Load Calculation

By the Editorial Team | Published: August 2026 | Last Updated: August 2026

The Step Between the Load Calculation and the Equipment Order

Manual S is the ACCA procedure for selecting a specific piece of equipment once the load is known. It sits between Manual J, which produces the load, and Manual D, which sizes the duct system. Skipping it is the single most common way a properly calculated load still ends up matched to the wrong equipment. The step is short, the math is not difficult, and the outputs are directly tied to whether the installed system will actually control temperature and humidity at design conditions.

The exercise is a matching one. The load calculation gives a sensible cooling requirement and a latent cooling requirement, both in Btu per hour at the local design conditions. The manufacturer publishes performance data for each condensing unit and indoor coil combination across a grid of outdoor and indoor temperatures. Manual S is the process of reading that grid at the design conditions for the location, then checking that the selected equipment sits within the ACCA-permitted oversizing window.

What Expanded Performance Data Actually Shows

Nameplate tonnage is a marketing number. Real equipment capacity varies with outdoor temperature, indoor return-air temperature, indoor return-air wet-bulb, and indoor airflow. Manufacturers publish this in expanded performance tables, sometimes called AHRI-certified or ARI performance data, and every ACCA-approved Manual S calculation reads from these tables at the design conditions rather than the AHRI rating point.

The AHRI rating point is 95 degrees Fahrenheit outdoor dry-bulb, 80 degrees Fahrenheit indoor dry-bulb, and 67 degrees Fahrenheit indoor wet-bulb, at a specified airflow. Real design days rarely match those exact conditions, and the actual delivered capacity will move up or down accordingly.

The values a Manual S calculation extracts from the table:

  • Total cooling capacity at the local design outdoor and indoor conditions.
  • Sensible cooling capacity at those same conditions, which is what actually cools the air.
  • Latent cooling capacity, calculated as total minus sensible, which is what removes moisture.
  • The airflow the coil requires to deliver those capacities.

The Oversizing Caps and Why They Exist

ACCA Manual S sets hard upper bounds on how far the selected equipment can exceed the calculated sensible load. For a straight-cool system, sensible capacity at design cannot exceed 115 percent of the sensible load. For a heat pump operating in cooling mode, the cap is 125 percent. The extra headroom for heat pumps recognizes that the same equipment has to satisfy heating loads too, and a small oversize helps at low ambient temperatures.

Above the cap, the system runs shorter and shorter cycles on typical days. Short cycles reduce runtime, and reduced runtime reduces the number of hours the coil is actively pulling moisture out of the air. In cooling-dominant climates the practical outcome is a house that is at temperature setpoint but reading 60 or 65 percent relative humidity, which occupants describe as clammy or muggy even though the thermostat reads 74 degrees.

The most common symptoms of a Manual S cap violation, in the order occupants notice them:

  • Indoor humidity readings above 55 percent during the cooling season, even at temperature setpoint.
  • Very short run cycles (under 10 minutes) on mild summer days.
  • Uneven room temperatures, because short cycles do not last long enough to distribute air through the duct system.
  • Higher-than-expected utility bills, driven by the compressor high current draw at startup relative to its short runtime.

Sensible Heat Ratio and Why It Matters

Sensible heat ratio, or SHR, is the fraction of a system total cooling capacity that goes to lowering air temperature rather than removing moisture. A high-SHR unit (0.80 or higher) is efficient at temperature reduction but weak at moisture removal. A low-SHR unit (below 0.75) is better suited to humid climates where the calculated latent load is a large fraction of the total.

Manual S requires the selected equipment SHR at design conditions to match the house calculated load SHR within an acceptable tolerance. A house with a 0.72 load SHR paired with a 0.82 SHR unit is a design mismatch even when the total capacity looks correct, because the unit will remove too little moisture.

Single-Stage, Two-Stage, and Variable-Speed Selection

The equipment class affects how the Manual S check plays out. Single-stage units either run at full capacity or off; they hit the sensible cap the fastest and are the hardest to size for humid climates. Two-stage units run at a reduced capacity most of the time and step up to full on hot afternoons, which softens the cycling penalty. Variable-speed or inverter-driven units modulate continuously between roughly 25 percent and 100 percent of nameplate capacity, which lets a designer size closer to the load without oversizing risk.

Is Manual S required by code or is it optional?

Manual S is referenced by the International Residential Code alongside Manual J and Manual D as the accepted procedure for equipment selection. Most state code adoptions incorporate the IRC references, which makes Manual S part of the required design workflow rather than an optional add-on. Enforcement varies by jurisdiction. The design-verification programs run by third parties, including the Energy Star Certified Homes and the ACCA Quality Installation standards, explicitly require documented Manual S selection.

Can nameplate tonnage be used instead of expanded performance data?

Nameplate tonnage is unsuitable for Manual S because it is measured at the AHRI rating point (95 degrees outdoor, 80 degrees indoor, 67 degrees wet-bulb) which almost never matches a specific location design conditions. A three-ton nameplate unit delivers less than 36,000 Btu/hr on a 100-degree afternoon and more on a 90-degree afternoon. Reading the manufacturer expanded performance table at the actual design conditions is the only way to know delivered capacity.

What happens when the selected equipment exceeds the 115 percent cap?

Selections above the 115 percent sensible cap trigger a flag in ACCA-approved Manual S software (Wrightsoft Right-Suite, Elite Rhvac, and Cool Calc all enforce it). Designers who accept the flag usually accept a documented dehumidification problem. In practice, occupants notice the humidity within the first cooling season, and the fix is either equipment replacement or the addition of a whole-house dehumidifier, either of which costs more than picking correctly the first time.

How does variable-speed equipment change the Manual S check?

Variable-speed equipment can modulate down to roughly 25 percent of nameplate capacity, which means short-cycling risk is dramatically lower even at the upper end of the oversizing window. A designer can select closer to the load without triggering the same humidity penalty a single-stage unit would produce. The Manual S check is still required and the caps still apply, but the practical consequence of sitting near the cap is much smaller for a modulating unit than for a single-stage unit.

Does Manual S apply to ductless mini-split systems?

Manual S applies to mini-splits, and the manufacturer expanded performance data is still the correct input. The additional consideration for ductless is that a multi-zone system connected indoor unit capacity often exceeds the outdoor unit simultaneous capacity, and the Manual S selection has to account for the diversity factor the manufacturer publishes. Skipping this step routinely produces mini-split installations that satisfy no zone properly at design conditions.

Where does the coil airflow fit into the selection?

Coil airflow is one of the axes on the expanded performance table, and every published capacity assumes a specific airflow across the coil. If the duct system cannot deliver that airflow, the actual sensible capacity drops and the latent capacity rises, which shifts the sensible heat ratio. Manual S selection includes verifying that the airflow the coil needs is what the duct system will actually provide, which loops the calculation back to Manual D.

How often does the selected model differ from what the contractor would have chosen by rule of thumb?

In the majority of retrofits, a proper Manual J and Manual S produces a smaller system than the outgoing unit, often by half a ton to a full ton. The rule of thumb almost always oversizes because it assumes the pre-modern envelope conditions the original industry rules were built around. On new construction the difference is smaller but usually still points to a smaller unit than the builder default.

Does the Manual S output name a specific model number?

The output names a specific outdoor unit and indoor coil combination, because performance data is only valid for matched combinations. Mixing an outdoor condenser with a coil it was not tested against invalidates the manufacturer performance table and voids the Manual S selection. The AHRI directory publishes the certified matched combinations and their expanded performance data.
