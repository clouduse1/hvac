Cooling Design for Hot-Humid Climate Zone 2A Homes

By the Editorial Team | Published: August 2026 | Last Updated: August 2026

Why Hot-Humid Climate Zones Break Cooling Design Assumptions

IECC Climate Zone 2A covers the Gulf Coast band of the United States: the lower half of Texas, most of Louisiana and Mississippi, southern Alabama, most of Florida, and coastal Georgia. It is the zone where residential cooling design assumptions built for dry or mixed climates start to fail visibly, because moisture is the binding constraint on comfort for most of the cooling season. A load calculation and equipment selection that would work fine in Zone 4A can produce a humidity-complaint installation in Zone 2A even when the math is technically correct.

The distinguishing feature of Zone 2A is not the peak temperature. Peak dry-bulb design temperatures in the zone typically sit between 92 and 98 degrees Fahrenheit, which is not extreme by U.S. standards. The distinguishing feature is the coincident wet-bulb temperature, which routinely runs 76 to 80 degrees during design conditions and pushes latent load to 30 percent or more of the total cooling load. That shifts almost every design decision.

What Changes in the Manual J Calculation

A Manual J run for a Zone 2A house uses the ACCA-listed 1 percent design dry-bulb and coincident wet-bulb for the specific city, not a rounded regional value. Representative city design conditions:

  • Houston, TX: 96°F dry-bulb / 77°F wet-bulb
  • Mobile, AL: 94°F dry-bulb / 78°F wet-bulb
  • New Orleans, LA: 93°F dry-bulb / 78°F wet-bulb
  • Orlando, FL: 93°F dry-bulb / 77°F wet-bulb
  • Miami, FL: 91°F dry-bulb / 79°F wet-bulb

Each of those pairs produces different sensible and latent split ratios, and equipment selection follows accordingly.

Infiltration is a bigger factor in Zone 2A than in cooler climates, because outdoor air brought in through leaks carries moisture that has to be removed indoors. A house with 5 air changes per hour at 50 pascals in a hot-humid climate imports a measurable latent load that the equipment has to overcome before it can start satisfying the sensible load. Blower-door testing to establish the actual infiltration rate, rather than defaulting to a table value, is one of the highest-leverage inputs to the calculation in this zone.

Equipment Selection Choices That Look Different Here

The equipment choices that work in a dry climate stop working in Zone 2A once latent load crosses roughly 25 percent of total load. Single-stage equipment sized to the sensible load leaves latent capacity uncovered. Oversized equipment satisfies the thermostat and shuts off before pulling humidity down. Variable-capacity equipment is the class most designers reach for first in this zone, because it can run long low-capacity cycles that keep the coil cold and moisture removal continuous.

The design considerations that shift most sharply in Zone 2A:

  • Sensible heat ratio (SHR) of the selected equipment should sit at or below 0.75 to match the house typical load SHR.
  • Airflow across the coil is often reduced below the 400 CFM per ton default (to roughly 350 CFM per ton) to increase moisture removal.
  • Whole-house dehumidifiers are frequently added as a second-stage moisture control for shoulder-season conditions when the AC does not run enough.
  • Duct systems in unconditioned attics carry a large penalty, because Zone 2A attics regularly hit 130 degrees and drive both conduction loss and latent gain from duct leakage.

The Attic-Duct Penalty

Ductwork located in an unconditioned attic is standard in Zone 2A construction and is one of the largest sources of hidden capacity loss. Even a well-sealed duct system loses meaningful capacity to attic temperatures, and a duct system with the code-typical 6 percent leakage rate loses substantially more. Bringing the ducts into conditioned space, or building an unvented sealed attic, is the design move that produces the largest single reduction in cooling load for many Zone 2A retrofits.

The Comfort Metric That Matters Most

In dry climates, the primary comfort metric is dry-bulb temperature at the thermostat. In Zone 2A, indoor relative humidity is the comfort metric that shows up first in occupant complaints. A house at 74 degrees and 45 percent RH feels comfortable; the same house at 74 degrees and 62 percent RH feels uncomfortable, and residents will lower the thermostat to compensate, which increases energy use without solving the underlying moisture problem.

Indoor condition Occupant experience Design implication
74°F / 45 percent RH Comfortable, no moisture complaint Target for correctly sized system
74°F / 55 percent RH Comfort edge; noticeable in cool rooms Acceptable ceiling per ACCA guidance
74°F / 62 percent RH Clammy; mold risk in wall assemblies System is oversized or has SHR mismatch
74°F / 70 percent RH Actively uncomfortable; visible condensation Design failure; add dehumidification or resize

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Climate Zone 2A treated differently than warmer Zone 1A?

Zone 1A (roughly southern Florida) has slightly higher latent loads and lower diurnal temperature swing, but the design approach is broadly the same as Zone 2A. Zone 2A is the larger and more diverse zone, spanning Houston, New Orleans, Mobile, Orlando, and Charleston, and it captures most of the U.S. hot-humid construction market. The design principles that apply in 2A also apply in 1A with minor tightening on latent-capacity margins.

Does a high-SEER system automatically handle humidity better?

SEER measures energy efficiency, not dehumidification performance. A high-SEER single-stage unit can be a poor dehumidifier because the efficiency comes from a large evaporator that produces a high sensible heat ratio. Two-stage and variable-speed units at similar SEER ratings usually handle humidity better because their run pattern favors long low-capacity cycles. The SEER number alone does not predict humidity outcomes in Zone 2A.

What indoor humidity range should a Zone 2A design target?

The industry-accepted range for occupant comfort and building durability is 45 to 55 percent relative humidity indoors during the cooling season. Below 45 percent is unusual to hit in this zone without active dehumidification and is not necessary for comfort. Above 55 percent starts to be noticeable, and above 60 percent creates conditions for mold growth in wall assemblies and behind furniture on exterior walls. ASHRAE Standard 55 uses this range as its thermal comfort envelope.

When is a supplemental dehumidifier the right call?

Supplemental dehumidifiers become a design choice when the house has shoulder-season conditions (spring and fall) where outdoor temperature is mild but outdoor dew point is high. In those weeks the AC does not run enough to control humidity, and indoor RH drifts up regardless of how well the AC is sized. Whole-house ducted dehumidifiers in the 90-to-130 pint per day range address this gap without oversizing the primary cooling equipment.

How much does bringing ducts into conditioned space actually save?

Moving ducts from an unconditioned vented attic into conditioned space typically reduces measured cooling energy use by 10 to 20 percent in Zone 2A homes, according to Building America research from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. The design-load reduction from the change is often enough to justify a smaller nominal tonnage on equipment replacement, which lowers first cost and improves humidity control simultaneously.

Does building tightness make the humidity problem worse?

Building tightness reduces the moisture load imported by infiltration, which makes the humidity problem easier to control, not worse. The misconception that tight houses have moisture problems usually traces to tight houses without properly designed ventilation, where indoor moisture generated by occupants accumulates. The correct pairing is a tight envelope plus a mechanical ventilation system with dehumidification, which is standard in ACCA Manual RS ventilation guidance.

Are the same equipment brands used in Zone 2A as in dry climates?

The same manufacturers ship into Zone 2A, but the model selection within each brand shifts toward the variable-speed and high-latent-capacity lines. Standard single-stage models designed for dry-climate applications are often not the best fit even from the same manufacturer. The Manual S selection process picks the right model within the brand rather than picking the brand itself.

Do heat pumps make sense in Climate Zone 2A?

Heat pumps are increasingly common in Zone 2A because heating loads are modest and can be satisfied by the same equipment used for cooling. A cold-climate heat pump is not required in this zone; standard-performance heat pumps handle the small winter heating loads efficiently. The primary design question is still cooling and dehumidification, which drives equipment selection more than the heating side.
